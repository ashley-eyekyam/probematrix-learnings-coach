Bottom line

  • Real competence gaps: specifying and testing pressure parts, recording calibration changes, and switching from locating a leak to isolating it.
  • Most of what went wrong is a system problem training cannot fix: no specification, no return-to-service procedure, no remote isolation, no isolation authority in the plan.
  • Control-room staff held emergency duties after a four-day general course with no defined qualification or simulator practice; a qualification standard is needed before more courses.
  • Teach to the level of the cause: crew modules for acts and conditions, supervisor and manager modules for checks, warnings and authority.

Modules

Module: Returning pressure equipment to service

Maintenance fitters and techniciansHalf a day

Objective

Given a pressure-boundary job, the learner names the part specification, the test, the test pressure and the witness, and stops the job when any of them is missing.

Content
  1. Case: a meter in service since 1986 was built for a rectangular-slot gasket; its specification was lost, and locally bought O-rings were fitted as regular practice with no specification or quality check.
  2. Case: after the last seal change the system was only brought to operating pressure; there was no hydro test to design pressure, and the meter was in use when it failed.
  3. Activity: sort sample seals into "matches the specification", "approved substitute" and "stop and ask", using the item's records.
  4. Activity: walk a return-to-service record and find what is missing.
  5. System note: this module works only with a written specification and a return-to-service procedure; where they are missing, the learner is taught to stop and ask, not to fill the gap.
  6. Competence standard: to be set by the competence system owner
  7. Refresher interval: to be set by the competence system owner
Assessment
  1. Practical: complete a return-to-service record for a simulated job, observed by an assessor.
  2. Questions: assessment items on specification and testing.

Module: Responding to a release you cannot reach

Control-room engineers and operators, security staffHalf a day, simulator or tabletop

Objective

In a simulated release that denies access, the learner reports "access denied", names the isolation points that can be reached safely and asks for the isolation decision without delay.

Content
  1. Case: security staff saw the cloud first; the control room heard of it as "some vapour cloud or steam leakage"; engineers tried to approach and were driven back; the first valve was closed by hand at 03:01.
  2. Case: the gas detectors only lit a panel and started no protective action.
  3. Activity: on the area drawing, mark which valves isolate each section and where each is worked from.
  4. Activity: practise the standard release message between a security post and the control room.
  5. System note: this module supports, and does not replace, remote isolation, executive gas detection and written isolation authority, which are system changes for manager-coach.
  6. Competence standard: to be set by the competence system owner
  7. Refresher interval: to be set by the competence system owner
Assessment
  1. Simulator or tabletop scenario, scored on the time to the isolation request and the content of the release message.
  2. Questions: assessment items on alarms and isolation.

Module: Calibration records that show what was done

Instrument techniciansTwo hours

Objective

The learner records every setting changed during a calibration, including changes reversed, with as-found and as-left values, and does not sign a record that leaves a change out.

Content
  1. Case: during a calibration in December 2019 the meter's range setting was changed and later changed back, with neither change on the signed records and no checklist.
  2. Activity: complete a calibration record from a worked example that includes a reversed change.
  3. System note: a calibration checklist for critical meters is a system change; until it exists, the learner uses the worked example as the record.
  4. Competence standard: to be set by the competence system owner
  5. Refresher interval: to be set by the competence system owner
Assessment
  1. Practical: an assessor reviews a completed calibration record for a critical meter.

Module: Accepting work back: hold points, notifications and permits

Maintenance and operations supervisorsHalf a day

Objective

The supervisor applies the return-to-service hold points: a recorded test with a witness, the part's specification reference, a closed notification and a closed permit, and declines to accept work that lacks them.

Content
  1. Case: the seal job ran on a maintenance notification with no job safety analysis; no one checked the part, the method or the test, and the notification was not closed or reviewed.
  2. Activity: coaching conversation on a job handed back late in the shift without a test record.
  3. Activity: map each hold point to the form where it should live (permit, return-to-service certificate, shift log or job card).
  4. System note: supervisors can hold the line only if the work-order system and management back them; those changes are for manager-coach.
  5. Competence standard: to be set by the competence system owner
  6. Refresher interval: to be set by the competence system owner
Assessment
  1. Observed coaching conversation, assessed against the hold points.
  2. Questions: assessment items on supervision and the levels of cause.

Module: Reading repeat failures and integrity warnings

Maintenance, integrity and planning managersTwo hours

Objective

The manager recognises a repeat failure or an unactioned inspection result as a reliability warning and routes it to a root-cause review or a fitness-for-service decision.

Content
  1. Case: the same meter's gasket was replaced three times in about 22 months with the same substitute part, and none of the repairs triggered a root-cause review.
  2. Case: pipe-wall loss found in 2017 had no corrective plan three years later, and pitting found in July 2019 on a zero-corrosion-allowance line was treated by painting.
  3. Activity: from a maintenance history extract, find the bad actors and decide the route for each.
  4. Competence standard: to be set by the competence system owner
  5. Refresher interval: to be set by the competence system owner
Assessment
  1. Case review: the manager presents the route chosen for each warning and why.

Module: Isolation authority and the emergency plan

Shift in-charges and emergency-response leadersHalf a day, tabletop

Objective

The senior shift leader states the isolation criteria, the valves and the delegated authority for each release scenario, and leads an isolation decision in a night-shift exercise without waiting for escalation.

Content
  1. Case: the plan named the hydrocarbon-leak scenario but not isolation; no one below the plant manager held standing authority to close the emergency valves; escalation ran one call at a time.
  2. Activity: tabletop exercise with injects from the night, run without senior staff present.
  3. System note: the criteria and authority must first be written into the plan by its owner; until then this module surfaces the gap rather than teaching an authority that does not exist.
  4. Competence standard: to be set by the competence system owner
  5. Refresher interval: to be set by the competence system owner
Assessment
  1. Tabletop exercise, assessed on the time to the isolation decision and the use of the criteria.

Scenario exercises

Scenario. Decision point 1: the original seal is not in stores and the meter is needed back. Decision point 2: the job is finished and nobody has written down a test pressure. Decision point 3: operations ask to put the meter back on line now.

Discussion prompts
  1. Facilitator note: let the group say what made the usual O-ring the sensible choice at the time before discussing alternatives.
  2. Facilitator note: ask what needs to exist (a specification, a procedure, a named witness) for "stop" to be the easy answer.
  3. Facilitator note: close on what the learner will ask for on the next job, not on blame.

Scenario. Decision point 1: a call reports "some vapour cloud" and the gas alarm sounds. Decision point 2: you go out and cannot see or approach the source. Decision point 3: the person who can authorise isolation is not on site.

Discussion prompts
  1. Facilitator note: pause at each decision point and ask what the learner knows at that moment, not what the investigation later found.
  2. Facilitator note: draw out which valves could be reached safely and who could be asked.
  3. Facilitator note: record the system changes the group names for the manager-coach list.

Scenario. Decision point 1: mid-calibration, a range setting has to be changed to complete a check. Decision point 2: the setting is put back. Decision point 3: the record is handed over for signature.

Discussion prompts
  1. Facilitator note: ask where the reversed change would be recorded today.
  2. Facilitator note: ask who would notice if it were not recorded, and when.

The seal in stores does not match the shape of the groove, and nobody can find the specification. What do you do?

Options
  • Fit it: it is what the crew normally uses
  • Stop and ask the supervisor for the specification or an approved substitute (correct)
  • Fit it and record it as a temporary repair
  • Fit it and test at operating pressure
Why it matters

Recognising a non-specified pressure part and stopping.

Answer

Stop and ask the supervisor for the specification or an approved substitute; do not fit the part on the strength of "what we normally use".

Good evidence looks like

The learner names who they would ask and where the specification should be recorded.

Why does pressurising a repaired joint to operating pressure not prove it?

Why it matters

Understanding the purpose of a test to design pressure.

Answer

Operating pressure does not prove the joint against the pressures it must withstand; a written test to the test pressure, with a witness, is what proves it.

Good evidence looks like

The learner names the test, the test pressure source and the witness.

What three things must be on the record before pressure-boundary equipment returns to service?

Options
  • The part's specification reference (correct)
  • A recorded test to the written test pressure (correct)
  • The witness's signature (correct)
  • The crew's shift roster
  • A note that the joint held at operating pressure
Why it matters

Knowing the return-to-service hold points.

Answer

The part's specification reference, a recorded test to the written test pressure, and the witness's signature.

Good evidence looks like

All three named without prompting.

You are about to replace the same gasket on the same meter for the third time. What should happen besides the repair?

Why it matters

Recognising a repeat failure as a reliability warning.

Answer

Record it as a repeat failure and raise a root-cause request, so the item is reviewed rather than repaired again unseen.

Good evidence looks like

The learner names where the repeat is recorded and who receives the request.

During calibration you change a range setting and later change it back. What goes on the record?

Why it matters

Keeping a complete calibration record.

Answer

Both changes, with as-found and as-left values; the signed record must show what was done, not only the end state.

Good evidence looks like

A completed example record that includes the reversed change.

On this site, what did a gas alarm on the annunciator panel do by itself?

Options
  • It shut down the plant
  • It started fire suppression
  • It only displayed the alarm; every protective action depended on people (correct)
  • It closed the isolation valves
Why it matters

Knowing what the alarm does and does not do.

Answer

It only displayed the alarm; it did not shut down the plant or start fire suppression, so every protective action depended on people.

Good evidence looks like

The learner can say which alarms on their unit trip equipment and which do not.

You go out to find a leak and the cloud stops you approaching. What is your next step?

Options
  • Keep looking for another way to the source
  • Stop approaching, tell the control room "access denied" and ask for isolation from points that can be reached safely (correct)
  • Wait at the edge of the cloud for the shift in-charge
  • Go back to the control room and watch the alarm panel
Why it matters

Switching from locating to isolating.

Answer

Stop approaching, tell the control room "access denied", and ask for isolation from the points that can be reached safely.

Good evidence looks like

The learner names the isolation points for their area and where they are worked from.

You are on a security post and see a white cloud near a process area. Whom do you call first, and what do you say?

Why it matters

Reporting a release so the control room can act.

Answer

The control room for that area, saying "hydrocarbon release", where it is and how big it is.

Good evidence looks like

The learner names the right control room for each area they watch.

True or false: a flameproof lamp that still lights is still flameproof, even with three bolts missing from its cover.

Options
  • True
  • False (correct)
Why it matters

Recognising a defect in explosion-protected equipment.

Answer

No. A flameproof fitting with missing bolts or an open cover is not flameproof; report it and have it made safe the same shift.

Good evidence looks like

The learner lists what to check on a flameproof fitting on rounds.

Level of cause: "Locally bought O-rings were fitted as regular practice because the original specification had been lost and no one owned re-establishing it." At which level does the main fix sit, and why?

Options
  • Level 1, unsafe acts
  • Level 2, preconditions
  • Level 3, supervision
  • Level 4, organisational influences (correct)
Why it matters

Placing a cause at its level (L1 to L4).

Answer

Level 4, organisational: the missing specification and owner are resource and policy conditions. The fix is an owner and a specification, not an instruction to the fitter.

Good evidence looks like

The learner separates the act (L1) from the organisational condition (L4).

Level of cause: "The maintenance notification was not closed or reviewed after the job." Which level is this, and what kind of fix does it need?

Options
  • Level 1, unsafe acts
  • Level 2, preconditions
  • Level 3, supervision (correct)
  • Level 4, organisational influences
Why it matters

Placing a cause at its level (L1 to L4).

Answer

Level 3, supervision: a check and correction that did not happen. The fix is a change to checks and oversight, such as a close-out review, not a reminder to the crew.

Good evidence looks like

The learner names a supervision-level fix.

Level of cause: "The vapour cloud stopped people approaching the isolation valves." Which level is this?

Options
  • Level 1, unsafe acts
  • Level 2, preconditions (correct)
  • Level 3, supervision
  • Level 4, organisational influences
Why it matters

Placing a cause at its level (L1 to L4).

Answer

Level 2, preconditions: the physical and technological environment at the time. It explains why the act of isolating was delayed, and points to a design change (remote operation).

Good evidence looks like

The learner links the precondition to an engineering fix.

Why is more training alone not enough to close the control-room competence gap seen here?

Why it matters

Understanding what training cannot fix.

Answer

There was no defined qualification and no simulator practice, and the valves and alarms needed people to act. Training supports, but does not replace, a qualification standard used for rostering and the engineering changes.

Good evidence looks like

The learner names one system change that must accompany the training.
