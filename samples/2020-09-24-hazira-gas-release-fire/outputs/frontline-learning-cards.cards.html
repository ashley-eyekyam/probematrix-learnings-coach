Fit only the seal the specification calls for

What happened

The condensate meter, in service since 1986, was built for a rectangular-slot gasket. Its specification was lost, so locally bought round O-rings were fitted as regular practice, with no specification, quality check or approval. The seal fitted on 18 Sep 2020 is the probable source of the release.

Why it made sense at the time

The job had to be done, and nothing in the system said stop: no specification, no approved substitute and no owner for either. Earlier changes had used the same kind of O-ring, so it felt normal.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: re-establish the seal specification, stock only certified parts against it, and have stores refuse to issue a pressure-boundary seal without a specification reference.

Warning signs

  • The part does not match the shape of the groove it goes into
  • Nobody can show you a specification for the seal
  • "This is what we normally use" is the only reason given

What good looks like

No specification, no fit: the crew asks the supervisor for the specification before fitting any seal on a pressure part.

Did you know?

  • No record shows when, or on whose authority, the site moved from the maker's gasket to a locally bought O-ring.

What can you do?

  • Check the part against the groove and the specification before you fit it.
  • If nobody can show a specification, ask your supervisor before fitting.

Questions for discussion

  • Which pressure parts here have no specification we can find?
  • Who do we ask when a spare differs from the original?
  • What would make pausing to ask easy?

Prove the joint before it goes back into service

What happened

After the gasket change on 18 Sep 2020, the system was only pressurised to operating pressure and isolated; no hydro test to design pressure was done. The meter went back online the next day and was in use when it failed.

Why it made sense at the time

No procedure covered calibration or return to service of these high-pressure meters, so the quickest available check became the normal one. Holding at operating pressure looked like proof that the joint was tight.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: a return-to-service procedure that sets the test, the test pressure and the witness, and a work-order system that holds the equipment until a test result is entered.

Warning signs

  • Nobody can tell you the test pressure for the joint you opened
  • The only test planned is "put it back on line and watch"
  • No one is named to witness the test

What good looks like

Every job that opens a pressure boundary ends with a recorded test to the written test pressure, witnessed by a second person, before operations accept the equipment.

Did you know?

  • The meter failed six days after the seal change, five days after it went back on line.

What can you do?

  • Before you hand back, ask for the written test pressure and the witness.
  • Write the test result on the job record; if no test was done, write that.

Questions for discussion

  • What test proves a joint we have just made?
  • Where is the result written, and who signs?
  • What if no test equipment is available?

Ask for the second check on a high-consequence job

What happened

The seal job on 18 Sep 2020 was planned and manned like routine work. No one was assigned to check the seal specification, its quality or the joint after it was made, so the person doing the job was the only check.

Why it made sense at the time

Pressure-boundary maintenance on hydrocarbon lines had not been classed as safety-critical, so no inspector, test witness or close-out reviewer was planned for it.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: a safety-critical maintenance category in work planning that adds inspection, test-witness and close-out steps by itself.

Warning signs

  • You are the only person who will look at the part, the method and the test
  • The job card for a pressure part looks the same as for routine work

What good looks like

On a job that could cause a major release, a second person checks the part, the method and the test, and the crew asks for that check if nobody comes.

Did you know?

  • The O-rings were bought with no specification or quality check, so nobody checked the part before it reached the job either.

What can you do?

  • On a job that could cause a major release, ask who checks the part, the method and the test.
  • If nobody is named, ask your supervisor for the check before the job closes.

Questions for discussion

  • Which of our jobs could cause a major release if one step goes wrong?
  • Who is the second pair of eyes on those jobs today?
  • How would you ask for a check without seeming unsure?

Say so when you are fixing the same fault again

What happened

The same meter had its gasket replaced three times in about 22 months, and a malfunction notification was raised on 17 Sep 2020. Each repair was treated as the first, and none led to a root-cause review.

Why it made sense at the time

Nothing in the maintenance system counted repeat failures on one item, and no reliability role was looking for the pattern, so each failure looked routine.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: an automatic repeat-failure trigger in the maintenance system that opens a root-cause investigation.

Warning signs

  • You have done this exact repair on this item before
  • The fix is the same part that failed last time

What good looks like

The crew writes "repeat failure" on the job record, and that note starts a root-cause review rather than another identical repair.

Did you know?

  • A malfunction notification for this meter was raised on 17 Sep 2020, one week before the release.
  • That notification was not closed after the job, so nobody reviewed the repair afterwards.

What can you do?

  • Write "repeat failure" on the job record and say how often you have seen it.
  • Ask your supervisor whether the item should be reviewed rather than repaired again.

Questions for discussion

  • Which items on our unit do we keep fixing?
  • Where on the job record would a repeat be noticed?
  • Who decides whether we repair again or replace?

A gas alarm is late news

What happened

The gas detectors only lit the alarm panel in the control room; they did not shut anything down. The first control-room alarm came at about 02:20, after the camera had recorded the leak and security staff had already seen the cloud.

Why it made sense at the time

The detection system was designed to inform, not to act, and its signals were not stored in the control system's history, so the alarm looked like any other display alert.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: gas detection that starts isolation by itself when gas is confirmed in the metering and gas terminal areas, and a stored history for every detector signal.

Warning signs

  • A gas alarm with no automatic action behind it
  • A report of vapour or noise arriving before any alarm

What good looks like

Crews treat a gas alarm as a confirmed release until proved otherwise, and know which alarms trip equipment and which wait for a person.

Did you know?

  • The camera recorded the leak starting at 02:12:50; the first control-room alarm came about seven minutes later.
  • The detection system had no way to shut down the plant or start fire protection by itself.

What can you do?

  • Find out which gas alarms on your unit trip equipment and which only display.
  • If you see or hear a release, report it at once; do not wait for the alarm.

Questions for discussion

  • Which gas alarms on our unit trip equipment by themselves?
  • What is the first thing you do when one sounds?
  • Who do you tell, and in what words?

If you cannot reach the source, think isolation from a distance

What happened

The control-room engineers went out to find the leak and were driven back by the dense cloud. Senior shift staff reached the gate but could not enter. The first isolation valve was closed by hand at 03:01, about 46 minutes after the first detection.

Why it made sense at the time

Normal leak response is to find and fix the leak. The isolation valves could only be worked at the valve, inside the cloud, and the emergency plan gave no isolation criteria or authority, so the crew kept trying to approach and kept calling up the chain.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: isolation decision aids for each area at the control-room console, remote-operated emergency valves, and written authority to isolate on defined criteria.

Warning signs

  • The cloud stops you getting near the source
  • You are phoning for permission while the cloud grows

What good looks like

As soon as access is denied, the crew reports "access denied" to the control room and switches to the isolation points it can reach safely.

Did you know?

  • The first valve was closed at 03:01, seven minutes before the fire started.

What can you do?

  • Learn which valves isolate your section and where each one is worked from.
  • When the cloud stops you, report "access denied" and move to an isolation point you can reach safely.

Questions for discussion

  • Which valves isolate our section, and where are they worked from?
  • What would you do if you could not get near them?
  • Who tells you that you may isolate?

Call the control room that can act, and say what you see

What happened

Security staff at a watch tower and at the gate saw the cloud at about 02:15. Their calls went to the security network, the fire station and another control room. The gas terminal control room heard of it between 02:18 and 02:20 as "some vapour cloud or steam leakage".

Why it made sense at the time

Security posts had no direct line to the gas terminal control room, and there were no standard words for a hydrocarbon release, so each relay checked the report and described it again.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: a direct line from perimeter posts and the camera room to each process control room, and a standard release message drilled jointly by security and operations.

Warning signs

  • You see vapour or hear a loud noise and do not know which control room covers that area
  • Your report is being passed on by someone who did not see it

What good looks like

The first call goes to the control room for that area and says "hydrocarbon release", where, and how big.

Did you know?

  • Two security posts saw the cloud at 02:15, but neither could call the gas terminal control room directly.

What can you do?

  • Know which control room covers each area you watch, and keep its number at your post.
  • Say "hydrocarbon release", where and how big, in your first call.

Questions for discussion

  • Which control room covers each area you watch?
  • What words would you use to report a release?
  • What stops you calling that control room directly today?

A flameproof fitting with a bolt missing is not flameproof

What happened

A flameproof lamp at the skid had three bolts missing and its glass case open by a few centimetres. The investigating committee named it as the probable ignition source. Nearby fittings were intact.

Why it made sense at the time

Electrical maintenance mainly answered breakdowns, with little planned inspection of flameproof fittings, so the missing bolts were not picked up and repaired.

What the organisation is changing

The Learnings Register recommends, for the organisation to decide: replace the old flameproof lamps in hazardous areas with certified fittings, and set up an inspection programme for flameproof equipment with a record for each item.

Warning signs

  • A missing bolt, an open cover or a loose gland on a fitting in a hazardous area
  • A fitting that still works, so nobody reports it

What good looks like

Anyone who sees a damaged flameproof fitting reports it, and it is made safe the same shift.

Did you know?

  • After the fire, a check of the nine flameproof lamps near the skid reported them all together as heat damage; the lamp with missing bolts was not singled out.

What can you do?

  • On your rounds, look for missing bolts, open covers and loose glands on flameproof fittings.
  • Report any you find at once, even if the fitting still works.

Questions for discussion

  • Where are the flameproof fittings on our rounds?
  • Who do we tell when one is damaged, and how fast is it made safe?
  • What stops us reporting a fitting that still works?

Bottom line

  • No specification, no fit: a seal that does not match the original is a change, not a spare. [FND-8C0BB623; EVD-8645]
  • A joint you opened is not proven until it has passed a recorded test to the written test pressure. [FND-212DC9B7; EVD-2894]
  • When the same fault comes back, say so on the job record; a repeat is a warning, not a routine job. [FND-69067A1C; EVD-1494]
  • On this site a gas alarm only informs, and it can come after people have seen the cloud: report a release at once, to the control room that can act. [FND-A245072B; FND-5AB5DFA3; EVD-8657; EVD-4286]
  • If a release stops you reaching the source, stop approaching and switch to isolation from where you can. [FND-F887999D; EVD-9612]
