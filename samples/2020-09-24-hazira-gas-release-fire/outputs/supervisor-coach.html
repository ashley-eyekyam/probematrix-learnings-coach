Bottom line

  • No specification, no start; no recorded test, no hand-back: the seal job ran without either, and the seal it fitted is the probable release source.
  • A substitute part that has become regular practice is still a change: ask for the change review before you authorise it.
  • Close the loop: a notification left open and a repeat repair left unescalated took away the last checks before the release.
  • On the night, isolation waited for escalation; supervisors need written criteria and authority to isolate, which the site has still to settle.
  • Put each check on the form where the work is released, so it becomes the easy way rather than a reminder.

Known problems

3

A repeat is a signal, not a routine. The same meter had its gasket changed three times in about 22 months, each time with the same substitute part, and none of the repairs was escalated for root cause. When the same item comes back a third time, raise a root-cause request with the maintenance planner and tell the engineering function, and do not accept the same fix again without that review.

+6 supporting

An inspection result needs a decision, not a coat of paint. Pitting and wall loss found on a zero-corrosion-allowance line in July 2019 were painted, with no review of whether painting was enough. When an inspection reports wall loss or pitting, send it to the integrity engineers for a fitness-for-service decision before the coating work is accepted.

+6 supporting

A defect on standby equipment matters most when it is needed. Two of four diesel fire-water pumps were under maintenance and one tripped after about two minutes, with no cause found or action recorded. Record a trip or failed start the same shift, raise it as a safety-critical defect, and escalate to the fire-protection owner when a pump stays unavailable.

+6 supporting

In an emergency

4

The findings show isolation delayed while the response escalated. These are questions for the site to settle, not authority supervisors already hold.

On a confirmed major hydrocarbon release, who on shift may isolate without calling up the chain, and on what criteria? Put to: Plant leadership (owner of the emergency plan)

Why it matters

The on-site plan named hydrocarbon leakage but gave no isolation criteria, and isolation authority was not allocated to the on-shift crew; the response escalated by phone while the cloud grew.

Good evidence looks like

Written isolation criteria and delegated authority for each release scenario, known to every shift and tested in a night-shift drill.

+6 supporting

Which valves isolate each section of the unit, and from where can they be worked if the area cannot be entered? Put to: Operations function

Why it matters

The emergency valves could only be closed by hand at the valve, and the release made the area unreachable.

Good evidence looks like

An isolation matrix for each area at the control-room console, showing which valves are local-only and the plan when access is denied.

+6 supporting

Should the shift treat any outside report of vapour or noise as a confirmed release until it is proved otherwise? Put to: Operations function, with the security function

Why it matters

The control room heard of the release as "some vapour cloud or steam leakage", after it had been relayed and re-described.

Good evidence looks like

A standing instruction and a standard release message, used in joint security and operations drills.

+5 supporting

Who on each shift is qualified and practised to lead an emergency isolation? Put to: HR, training and competence function

Why it matters

Control-room staff held emergency duties after general training only, with no defined qualification or simulator practice.

Good evidence looks like

A defined control-room qualification used as a rostering rule, with a record per shift.

+6 supporting

Coaching conversations

3

Scenario. A fitter tells you the high-pressure meter is ready to box up. The original seal is not in stores, so the crew used the O-ring they always use. Operations want the meter back on line in the morning, and nobody can tell you the test pressure.

Discussion prompts
  1. What do you check before you sign the job?
  2. What do you say to operations about the morning?
  3. Who do you call for the specification and the test pressure?
  4. How do you make sure the next crew does not face the same choice?

Scenario. A notification comes in for a meter malfunction. Looking at the history, you see the same meter's gasket has been changed twice before in under two years, and the last notification for it was never closed.

Discussion prompts
  1. Is this a repair job or a reliability problem?
  2. Who do you escalate it to, and what do you ask for?
  3. What would you want closed before this job starts?

Scenario. At night the control room gets a call about "some vapour cloud or steam leakage" near the metering area. The gas alarm sounds a moment later. Your engineers go out and come back saying they cannot get near the source.

Discussion prompts
  1. What is your first decision, and who makes it?
  2. Which valves would isolate the release, and can anyone reach them?
  3. Whom do you call, and while you wait, what do you do?
  4. What would you want the emergency plan to have told you?
