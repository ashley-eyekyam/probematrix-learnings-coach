Emergency Response Learnings: Gas terminal release and fire, 2020

Emergency response: fire and fire-water teams, emergency planners, shift and site controllers, security and camera-room staff · Hazira gas terminal condensate release and fire · 2020-09-24 · run 2020-09-24-hazira-gas-release-fire · generated 2026-10-21

Bottom line

  • About 46 minutes passed from the first sighting to the first isolation; the plan gave no isolation criteria and no one on shift held standing authority to isolate.
  • The isolation valves could only be closed by hand, inside the cloud they were meant to stop.
  • People saw the release before any alarm, but the warning reached the control room that could act late and in softened words.
  • Gas detection only informed: it alarmed after the cloud was seen and started no protective action.
  • Fire protection held because the electrical pumps kept running; the diesel back-up and the drill records would not have shown the gap in time.

Learnings by response phase

11

Detection and alarm

If fire and gas detection only lights a panel, every protective step waits for people to notice, check and decide. Owner: Instrumentation and fire-protection engineering

The detectors were configured to the annunciator panel only, with no history in the control system and no automatic shutdown or suppression. The first control-room alarm came after the camera had recorded the leak and after security staff had seen the cloud, so detection added little time to the response.

Evidence: corroborated

One panning camera recorded the start of the release but alerted no one, and its record ended when the fire started. Owner: Security and surveillance function

A single pan-and-tilt camera with a slow pan gave the only view of the skid. It recorded the leak at onset but had no alarm function, and its cable burned through seconds after ignition. The coverage risk had not been assessed.

Evidence: direct
+6 supporting

Communication and escalation

People saw the release first, but their warnings reached the control room that could act late and in softened words. Owner: Security function with operations

Two security posts saw the cloud at about 02:15. Their calls went first to the security network, the fire station and another control room, and the gas terminal control room was told of "some vapour cloud or steam leakage" at about 02:18. Each relay checked the report and described it again; there was no direct line and no standard release message.

Evidence: corroborated
+6 supporting

Escalation ran up the chain one call at a time, and the chain had to be climbed before anyone isolated. Owner: Owner of the emergency plan

The overall shift in-charge informed senior management one after another rather than through a parallel call-out, and the site-controller role was handed over during the event. The plan's structure, not the people on shift, set the pace.

Evidence: direct
+8 supporting

Authority and decision to isolate

If no one on shift holds standing authority to isolate, isolation waits for the call that grants it. Owner: Plant leadership

No role between the shift in-charge and the plant manager held standing authority to close the emergency isolation valves, and isolation authority was not allocated to the on-shift crew. People reasonably believed they needed permission to shut in a major supply.

Evidence: corroborated
+8 supporting

When the cloud denies access, the response has to switch from "find the leak" to "isolate from a distance", and nothing prepared the shift for that switch. Owner: Operations function

The control-room engineers and then the shift in-charges tried to approach and were driven back by the gas. First isolation came at 03:01 and the 36-inch control valve at 03:04. This is a learning about decision support, not about the people who responded.

Evidence: corroborated
+3 supporting

Emergency isolation valves that can only be closed at the valve are out of reach in exactly the event they exist for. Owner: Operations and engineering functions

The gas terminal emergency valves had no remote operation from the control room, and the risk that a release would make them inaccessible had not been assessed. The first valve was closed by hand once the shift in-charge reached it.

Evidence: corroborated
+3 supporting

Plan coverage

The emergency plan named the hydrocarbon-leak scenario but not the decision it needed: when to isolate, which valves, and who decides. Owner: Owner of the emergency plan

The on-site plan included guidance for hydrocarbon leakage but not isolation or stoppage of facilities and pipelines, and the regulated plan's major-leak scenarios did not mention the isolation decision. Each responder had to improvise.

Evidence: direct
+8 supporting

Drill records with no observations or actions cannot show whether readiness is improving, or reveal a gap such as missing isolation criteria. Owner: Fire and emergency function

Monthly fire drill reports held dates but no actions or recommendations, and a separate data set was kept outside the approved guidelines. The fire team performed well on the night; the learning is about what the drills could not show.

Evidence: direct
+5 supporting

Fire-water and fire-protection readiness

Fire protection depended on the electrical fire-water pumps surviving the event, because three of four diesel pumps were unavailable or unreliable. Owner: Fire-protection function

Tank level and header pressure stayed healthy and electrical pumps carried the cooling. But two diesel pumps were under maintenance and one tripped after about two minutes, with no cause found or action recorded.

Evidence: direct
+8 supporting

Command structure

The people expected to lead the first response had general training only, so a rare, time-critical event was their first practice. Owner: HR, training and competence function

Control-room training was a four-day general course, with no defined qualification and no simulator practice, and the night-shift crew had no member with practised emergency-isolation competence.

Evidence: direct

Drill scenarios

3

Scenario. Night shift, with no senior staff on site. The exercise starts with a report from a perimeter security post.

Discussion prompts
  1. Measure: time from the first report to the isolation decision, and to the first valve closed, against a time standard the site sets from its consequence study.
  2. Debrief: record each observation and one action in the drill record.
Injects
  1. +00:00A security post reports a roar and a white cloud near the metering skid.
  2. +00:05The gas alarm sounds on the control-room alarm panel.

    Decision point Who decides to isolate, and on what criterion?

  3. +00:10The engineers who went out report that the cloud stops them reaching the source.

    Decision point Which valves isolate the section, and which can be reached safely?

  4. +00:20The shift in-charge is still on the way from quarters.

Scenario. A joint security and operations drill, started from a perimeter watch tower.

Discussion prompts
  1. Measure: time from first sighting to the process control room receiving a release message in the standard words.
Injects
  1. +00:00A watch-tower post sees vapour in an area covered by a process control room.

    Decision point Whom does the post call first?

  2. +00:02The camera room can see the cloud on screen.

    Decision point What words carry the size and place of the release?

  3. +00:03The process control room has had no alarm.

Scenario. A fire-cooling exercise on the fire-water system.

Discussion prompts
  1. Measure: time to establish cooling water from the diesel pumps alone.
  2. Debrief: record the trip cause, or record that it is not known, and the action.
Injects

  2. +00:05One diesel pump is under maintenance, and another trips shortly after starting.

    Decision point How is cooling kept up, and who decides?
