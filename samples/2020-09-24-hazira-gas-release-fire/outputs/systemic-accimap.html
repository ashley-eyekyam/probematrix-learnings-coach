The management-of-change procedure existed at company level but was not in use, so part and material substitutions reached the work site with no review.

Two company-level codes carry it down the map: OIC 2.1 links to routine violations at level 5 (5 mentions) and OIC 2.7 to misapplied procedures at level 5 (5 mentions). The procedure was published in 2016 and no change was put through it after 2018 [].

Evidence: corroborated

No procedure for calibrating high-pressure meters and returning them to service existed at the operational management level, so the work at the meter had no checklist or test to follow.

SVI 1.4 at level 4 links to checklists not followed at level 5 and to unclear technical guidance at level 6, 6 mentions each. The gasket was replaced and the meter returned to service with no test above operating pressure [].

Evidence: corroborated

Safety audits at company level used generic checklists, so assurance could not see plant-specific conditions at the work site.

OIC 2.7 at level 3 links to checklists not followed at level 5 (5 mentions), and supervisory oversight at level 4 (SVI 1.1) to the same code (6 mentions). The 2018 and 2019 internal audits kept no filled checklists [].

Evidence: corroborated

OIC 2.1 at level 3 and maintenance at level 6 (PTE 2.12) both link to routine violations at level 5 (5 and 4 mentions). The seal was replaced three times in 22 months [].

Evidence: corroborated

The company's emergency plan named the major-leak scenario but gave no isolation criteria, and the gap carried through the operational guidance to the people responding.

OIC 2.1 and OIC 2.7 at level 3 each link to SVI 1.4 at level 4 (4 mentions each), and SVI 1.4 links to unclear technical guidance at level 6. The plan covered leaks but not isolation or stoppage [].
